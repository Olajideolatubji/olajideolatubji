## Cold Open — The Vanishing

In October 2017, a woman boarded a budget flight from Bulgaria to Greece. Flight time: one hour and twenty-five minutes. The plane landed safely. Every passenger walked off. Except, somehow… her. No camera caught her leaving. No border recorded her crossing. Nobody — not Interpol, not the FBI, not her own family — has seen her since.

The FBI will pay you five million dollars if you can find her. And here's what should genuinely worry you: of the five frauds in tonight's ledger… hers isn't even the biggest.

Five companies. Five leaders the world called geniuses. A combined value of over one hundred billion dollars — every cent of it invented. We're going to count it, entry by entry, and by the end of this video you'll know exactly where that woman fits… and why she might never be found. This is the Red Ledger. Let's open it.

## Entry One — FTX ($32B)

Let's start with a question. How long does it take to destroy a thirty-two billion dollar company? A year? A quarter? Try nine days. And to understand how, you need to meet the man everyone trusted.

Sam Bankman-Fried was the good billionaire. Slept on a beanbag. Drove a Corolla. Said he was only getting rich so he could give it all away. He ran FTX, one of the biggest crypto exchanges on Earth. Politicians took his money. Celebrities did his adverts. A stadium wore his name. Trust me, he said. And everyone did.

Here's what "trust me" was hiding. Customers sent FTX billions to trade crypto. And next door — literally in the same office complex — sat Alameda Research, Sam's private trading firm. Between the two companies was a secret back door, written directly into the code. Customer money flowed in one side. Alameda gambled it out the other.

Eight billion dollars of other people's money — into bad bets, Bahamas penthouses, and political donations. Now — the nine days.

Day one: a leaked balance sheet shows Alameda is stuffed with funny money. Day two: a rival CEO tweets he's selling. Day three: customers panic and demand withdrawals — six billion dollars in seventy-two hours. Day six: withdrawals freeze. There is nothing left to withdraw. Day nine: FTX files for bankruptcy, and Sam's thirty-two billion dollar empire is worth exactly nothing.

Sam's personal fortune went with it — sixteen billion dollars, erased in under a week. The fastest personal wealth wipeout in modern history. And the man brought in to manage the wreckage was John Ray — the same specialist who had cleaned up the corpse of Enron. Remember that name. It's coming later in this ledger. Ray had spent forty years walking through corporate disasters, and he told Congress he had never seen, in his entire career, "such a complete failure of corporate controls." Worse than Enron. His words.

He was convicted on seven counts of fraud. Twenty-five years. The good billionaire turned out to be an ordinary thief with world-class PR.

Entry one, logged. But keep the missing woman in mind — because at least FTX had a product that actually worked. The next company's entire technology… was theatre.

## Entry Two — Theranos ($9B → running total $41B)

Every magic trick has three parts. The pledge, the turn… and the prestige. Elizabeth Holmes performed all three — on the entire American medical system.

The pledge: a machine called the Edison, the size of a printer, that could run hundreds of lab tests from one drop of blood. No needles. No labs. A revolution. Holmes dropped out of Stanford at nineteen to build it, and by thirty she was the world's youngest self-made female billionaire. On paper.

The turn: investors handed her seven hundred million dollars. Walgreens installed the machines in real pharmacies for real patients. Her board included two former Secretaries of State. Nine billion dollars of value, resting on one question that somehow nobody asked hard enough: does the box actually work?

And Holmes played the part completely. The black turtleneck, borrowed from her idol Steve Jobs. The unblinking stare. Even the voice — colleagues later said she deliberately spoke an octave lower than her natural voice, a slow baritone built for boardrooms, and that it would slip when she got tired. Every detail of the founder was engineered. Except the product.

It did not work. And here's the prestige — the part of the trick where they swap the box. When your blood went into a Theranos clinic, it didn't go into the Edison at all. It went out the back, to ordinary commercial machines bought from other companies — the exact machines Theranos claimed to replace. Real patients made real medical decisions — cancer scares, medication changes — off numbers from a magician's misdirection. Healthy patients were told their results looked cancerous. Sick patients were told they were fine. One test for a blood-thinning drug — where a wrong number can kill — had to be voided entirely. Every single result, thrown out.

In 2015 a Wall Street Journal reporter started pulling the thread. Theranos sued, threatened, and surveilled its own whistleblowers — some of them barely out of university. The thread kept coming. Regulators banned Holmes from her own lab. By 2018, Theranos was ash. She got eleven years. The turtleneck, it turns out, was the only part that was real.

Two entries. Forty-one billion. And a woman still on a plane that landed without her. Stay with me — because Theranos only faked a machine. The next company faked something much simpler. The money itself.

## Entry Three — Wirecard (€24B → running total $67B)

This one has a detail the others don't. When journalists exposed FTX and Theranos, their governments listened. When journalists exposed Wirecard… the German government investigated the journalists.

Wirecard was Germany's tech miracle — a payments company so successful it kicked a hundred-year-old bank out of the DAX, Germany's elite stock index. Twenty-four billion euros. The auditors signed off, year after year. There was just one detail. Almost two billion euros of Wirecard's cash — a quarter of everything it claimed to have — supposedly sat in two banks in the Philippines.

For five years, reporters at the Financial Times published story after story saying the numbers didn't add up. And for five years, Wirecard called it a conspiracy — and Germany's financial regulator agreed. It banned bets against Wirecard's stock and filed criminal complaints… against the reporters.

Trust me — enforced by the state. It went further than paperwork. The regulator imposed the first single-stock short-selling ban in German history — making it illegal to bet against the fraud. Reporters were followed. Sources were surveilled. One journalist described being watched by operatives hired through Wirecard's security network. The company under investigation was running counter-intelligence on the people investigating it.

Then, in June 2020, the auditors did the one thing they'd never done in a decade. They rang the banks in the Philippines and asked, directly: is the money there? The reply: we have never heard of this money. One point nine billion euros. It didn't exist. It had never existed.

Wirecard collapsed in a week — the first DAX company in history to go bankrupt. The CEO was arrested. But the chief operating officer, Jan Marsalek — the man who ran the fake half of the business — got in a car, crossed the border, and vanished. He's on Interpol's most wanted list to this day. And the trail investigators have pieced together since is stranger than the fraud itself: reporting by German and international outlets traced his escape route east, through Belarus, to Moscow — and alleges the man running Wirecard's fake billions had been working with Russian intelligence for years. A fraudster who may also have been a spy. That part is still unproven in court. The missing one point nine billion is not.

But even Wirecard was lying about one number in one account. The next company didn't lie about a number. It built an industrial machine for manufacturing lies — and won awards for it.

## Entry Four — Enron ($90 → $0.26 → running total $168B)

Enron was the seventh-largest company in the United States — an energy giant Fortune magazine crowned "America's Most Innovative Company" six years running. Which, in fairness, was accurate. Nobody has ever innovated fraud like Enron. Let me give you the tour.

Machine one: mark-to-market accounting. Sign a deal today — a power plant, a gas contract, anything — then guess what it might earn over the next twenty years, and book every dollar of that imaginary future profit right now, today. If the deal later loses money? Doesn't matter. The profit's already on the books. Enron wasn't reporting income. It was reporting daydreams.

Machine two: the maze. Because real losses were piling up, the finance chief built a network of thousands of shell companies and quietly buried the debt inside it. The losses vanished into the maze. The share price climbed to ninety dollars. Employees poured their pensions into it. Wall Street's smartest analysts rated it a strong buy.

And the culture matched the accounting. When California's power grid buckled in 2000 and 2001, recorded calls later caught Enron traders celebrating the blackouts — asking power plants to find reasons to shut down during shortages, then selling electricity back into the starving market at multiples of the price. Grandmothers sitting in the dark were a revenue strategy. The company's actual advertising slogan, at the time, was: "Ask why." Nobody did.

Then, in 2001, one executive resigned without explanation. One employee — Sherron Watkins — wrote a memo warning the company would, quote, implode in a wave of accounting scandals. One small loss got disclosed. Ninety dollars became eighty. Then forty. Then ten. Then… twenty-six cents.

Twenty thousand people lost their jobs. Thousands lost their entire retirement — because they'd been told, right up until the end: trust me. The collapse was so violent it killed Enron's auditor, one of the five biggest accounting firms in the world, which had spent the final weeks shredding the evidence. It literally changed American law.

Now. Every company in this ledger so far had something real at the centre — an exchange, a lab, offices, power plants. The final entry had none of that. The last company tonight was one hundred percent imaginary. And it's hers.

## Entry Five — OneCoin ($4B+ → running total $172B)

Her name is Dr. Ruja Ignatova. Oxford-educated. Ex-McKinsey. Ballgowns, diamond earrings, red lipstick. In 2014, at the height of Bitcoin mania, she stood on stage and announced the future of money: OneCoin. The Bitcoin killer.

She filled Wembley Arena. She told the crowd that in two years, nobody would even remember Bitcoin. People in a hundred and seventy-five countries believed her — pensioners, farmers, families selling land and cattle to buy in. At least four billion dollars. Probably far more.

Now the part that separates OneCoin from everything else tonight. Bitcoin — whatever you think of it — is real technology. A blockchain, running across thousands of computers, that nobody can secretly edit. OneCoin's blockchain did not exist. At all. There was no technology. There was a website, and behind the website, an ordinary database. When OneCoin "mined" your coins… an employee typed a bigger number into a spreadsheet.

And here's how you sell a coin that doesn't exist: you never sell the coin. You sell education. OneCoin's product was "trader packages" — folders of plagiarised course material, costing anywhere from a hundred euros to over a hundred thousand — which happened to come with tokens for the imaginary currency. Recruit a friend, take a commission. Recruit ten, build a downline. It wasn't a tech company with a pyramid problem. It was a pyramid wearing a tech company's clothes.

Four billion dollars. For rows in a spreadsheet.

By October 2017, US charges were being prepared, and Ruja knew it. So — the airport. Sofia to Athens, one hour twenty-five minutes, the flight from the start of this video. She boarded. The plane landed. And Dr. Ruja Ignatova stopped existing.

No sightings. No arrest. No body. Her co-founder got twenty years. Her own brother testified against her. Investigators have hunted her across Europe, the Gulf, and the Mediterranean for nearly a decade. Some believe powerful people are protecting her. Some believe she was killed by the very criminals who helped launder her billions, her body dropped from a yacht into the Aegean Sea. The FBI still lists her, still offers five million dollars, and still marks the file the same way: whereabouts unknown.

There have been claimed sightings over the years — a restaurant in Athens, a luxury flat in Dubai, a yacht in the Mediterranean. Every one investigated. Not one confirmed. One investigation aired the claim that she was murdered on a drug lord's orders back in 2018, her body dumped at sea — also never proven.

The honest answer is nobody knows. Somewhere out there is either a grave nobody's found… or a woman with four billion dollars who beat the entire world.
